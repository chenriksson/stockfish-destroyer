# Grandmaster Review — Destroyer 0.2.0 vs Stockfish 2000 (Draw, threefold repetition)

PGN: `games/2026-09-25T123624_sf2000_draw.pgn` · Evals: `games/analysis/2026-09-25T123624_sf2000_draw.evals.json`

## 1. Verdict

We should have lost this game outright, and the fact that it's a draw says more about Stockfish's imposed weakness than about our defense. White (us) spent the first nine moves shuffling queenside/kingside pawns to the third rank (a3,b3,c3,d3,e3,f3,g3,h3) without developing a single piece — a direct symptom of material-only evaluation with no development or center-control term. Black built an ideal classical center and, by move 18, had already forced our king to the center, stripped both sides of castling rights, and won the exchange through a clean pin-and-fork combination. From there our engine kept adding to the deficit by grabbing loose pawns (Bxg7) while its own king sat under direct attack, then in a totally dead position picked a string of essentially random moves instead of the objectively best defensive tries. The engine reached roughly –14 to –16 (i.e., mate-bound) around move 50-55. It survived only because Stockfish-at-2000 repeatedly failed to find the cleanest continuations, and the game eventually degenerated into a Rook+Bishop vs Queen ending where Black couldn't find a winning try and both sides shuffled into a threefold repetition. This is a lucky escape, not a save.

## 2. Critical moments

### Move 10, White to play — `c4` (best: `Ne2`)
FEN: `r2qkb1r/1pp2ppp/p1n1bn2/3pp3/P7/1PPPPPPP/8/RNBQKBNR w KQkq - 1 10`

We've played nine moves and every White piece is still on its home square; all eight pawns have left rank 2. Black, meanwhile, has a full classical center (d5/e5), Nc6, Nf6, Be6 all developed, and is one tempo from finishing development with ...Bc5 and long castling. This is the real turning point of the game, even though no single move here crosses the 100cp "mistake" bar — it's a slow bleed across nine moves, not one blunder. **What I'd play:** `Ne2`, getting a piece into the game and preparing d3-d4 to contest the center before Black finishes developing. **What we played:** `c4`, yet another pawn move that grabs space but develops nothing and further commits the pawn structure while the king is still home. A human doesn't play a ninth consecutive pawn move with all pieces asleep; the position doesn't call for more space, it calls for pieces. (This exact disease — no incentive to develop because material-only eval treats a home-square knight and a centralized knight identically — is already fixed by v2's tapered PST, so nothing further to flag here, but it's worth naming as the actual root cause of everything that follows.)

### Move 14, White to play — `dxe4` (best: `Nf3`)
FEN: `r2qk2r/1pp2ppp/p1n1b3/2b5/P1PpnP2/1P1P3P/8/RNBQKBNR w KQkq - 0 14`

This is the single biggest jump (–148cp) and the moment the game is actually lost. Black's knight sits on e4; recapturing with the pawn looks automatic, but it rips open the center and the long diagonal while our king is still on e1 and completely undeveloped — exactly the moment tactics land. **What happened next:** 14...Qh4+ 15.Ke2 (castling rights gone) d3+! (the pawn checks the king diagonally from d3) 16.Qxd3 Qf2+ 17.Kd1 Rd8!, pinning the queen on d3 to the king along the d-file. 18.Qxd8+ Kxd8 nets Black the exchange (queen for rook) and a permanently exposed White king. **What I'd play:** `Nf3`, attacking the e4 knight and developing instead of reflexively recapturing. It doesn't win the pawn back cleanly, but it keeps the center semi-closed, gets a piece out, and — critically — doesn't hand Black a forcing check sequence while our king has nowhere to go. The lesson for a human is instinctive: never open the center with your king still at home and your pieces still in the box, especially when the opponent has an open diagonal to h4.

### Move 25, White to play — `Bxg7` (best: `Ke1`)
FEN: `2kr4/1pp2ppp/p1b5/2b2P2/PnP1P3/1P2qN1P/1B1NB2R/R2K4 w - - 9 25`

Already dead lost (–767 before the move), but the pattern here recurs all game: our king on d1 is under fire from Black's queen (e3), knight (b4), and two bishops, and instead of consolidating we go pawn-hunting with `Bxg7`. **What I'd play:** `Ke1`, tucking the king toward the kingside rook and starting to untangle, because a piece up or down doesn't matter when mate is in the air — safety comes first. A grabbed g7-pawn is worthless if the king walks into a mating net two moves later. This isn't really about the evaluation score at this point (the game was already lost), it's diagnostic: material-only eval has no way to notice that its own king is the thing actually worth points here.

### Moves 46–57, White to play — `Bf1` / `Ra2` / `Rh2` / `Rh1` (best: `Rb2` / `Kh2` / `b4` / `Be2`)
Representative FEN (move 46): `1k6/1p3p1p/p7/2p5/P1P2n2/1P4q1/6B1/1R5K w - - 2 46`

By now the game is a formality (eval around –11 to –17), but this cluster is instructive: four separate moments where the engine had a genuinely useful defensive/consolidating try available (`Rb2` covering the second rank against checks, `Kh2` stepping out of a check pattern, `b4` creating counterplay/closing a file, `Be2` covering key squares) and instead played a move that accomplishes nothing (`Bf1`, `Ra2`, `Rh2`, `Rh1`), each costing another 100–430cp. **What's going on:** once material-only eval sees a position as "equally lost" everywhere, there's no signal left to prefer the move that actually makes the opponent's job harder. A human in a completely lost position still fights for the *practical* try — the move that gives the opponent the most chances to go wrong — rather than shuffling a piece to a square where it does nothing. This cluster is exactly why the position could have been finished off much sooner than it was.

### Moves 60–67 — the accidental escape
By move 62 (`Rxf5`) the material is Rook + Bishop + pawn (c4) for us vs Queen + 2 pawns (a5, c5) for Black — technically winning for Black, but not trivial to convert, especially against a shortened clock and imperfect play. From here both sides essentially mark time: White shuffles the bishop `Bf1`–`Be2`–`Bf1`, Black shuffles the king `Kb8`–`Kc7`–`Kb8`, and the position repeats three times. **What I'd play as White here:** honestly, close to what we did — with no way to create threats, shuffling and hoping for a repetition or a Black error is the correct practical try. **What Black should have done:** activate the king (`Kc6`–`d5`) or push the a-pawn while the queen restrains our rook/bishop coordination, rather than repeating. The draw here isn't a defensive achievement on our part — it's Stockfish-at-2000 failing to convert a straightforward technical win. Note this version (0.2.0) had no repetition detection at all, so even the escape into the threefold was blind luck rather than a chosen drawing strategy; v3's contempt term (seek repetition when losing) will make this an intentional resource going forward rather than an accident.

## 3. Recurring weaknesses in our engine's play

- **No development incentive in the opening.** Nine consecutive pawn moves with zero piece development, because material-only eval can't tell a knight on g1 from a knight on f3. (Already fixed by v2's tapered PST; validated here as the actual root cause of the whole game.)
- **Reflexive material-grabbing regardless of king safety.** `dxe4` and `Bxg7` both take free material while ignoring an immediate or looming attack on our own king — the eval has no concept that the king itself is worth defending independent of material count.
- **Chronic exposed-king shuffling once castling rights are lost.** Kd1→Kc1→Kc2→Kc1, then later Kg1/Kh1/Kf2 repeatedly, with no coordinated piece support — the king wanders because nothing in the eval rewards giving it real shelter.
- **No sense of "practical try" once clearly lost.** In a dead-lost position the engine treats all losing moves as roughly interchangeable and sometimes picks the one that loses fastest (the Bf1/Ra2/Rh2/Rh1 cluster) instead of the one that makes the opponent work hardest — costing free half-points against imperfect opposition like weakened Stockfish.
- **The eventual draw was accidental, not earned.** No repetition awareness in this version means the escape into the threefold was luck, not design (already addressed in v3).

## 4. Concrete suggestions (ranked by expected impact)

1. **Extend quiescence search to include direct checking moves, not just captures.** The decisive blunder of the game (`dxe4`, ply 27, –148cp) was refuted by a non-capturing check sequence (`Qh4+` ... `d3+`) that a capture-only quiescence search cannot see — the position looks "quiet" the instant a check is off the table for search purposes, even though a forced, material-winning sequence starts right there. Concretely: in `quiescence()`, after generating captures, also generate direct checks (one extra ply, only when the side to move has checks available and the static eval is within some margin of alpha/beta) so forcing check-based tactics like this are visible before the engine commits to opening the position. This is distinct from "check extensions" in the main search (already queued) and directly targets this game's worst decision.

2. **Prioritize the queued transposition table + hash move, killer/history heuristics, and PVS + LMR.** (Already queued — this game is strong evidence for it.) The `dxe4` miss and the whole Bf1/Ra2/Rh2/Rh1 cluster of "does-nothing" moves in a lost position are classic symptoms of shallow effective depth from weak move ordering inside a fixed 5s budget. These queued items should meaningfully reduce both categories.

3. **When implementing the queued king-safety (pawn shield) term, make it attacker-aware, not just structural.** Score king danger based on the number and proximity of enemy pieces (queen/knight/rook) within a few squares of the king and open files/diagonals leading to it — not only pawn-shield integrity. A pure pawn-shield term wouldn't have stopped `Bxg7` (ply 49): our shield was fine, the problem was Black's queen and knight sitting right next to our king. This is a specific refinement to the already-queued item, worth calling out to the implementer explicitly.

4. **Add a cheap mobility term (legal-move-count differential) as a tie-breaker.** New, not on the current queue. When material and (eventually) other eval terms are equal or the position is already decided, a mobility differential is nearly free to compute and would break ties in favor of active, flexible moves — directly addressing the Bf1/Ra2/Rh2/Rh1 cluster where multiple candidate moves looked identical to material-only eval but were not remotely equal in practical value.
