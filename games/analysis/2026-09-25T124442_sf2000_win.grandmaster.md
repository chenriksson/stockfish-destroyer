# Grandmaster Analysis — WIN vs Stockfish 2000 (2026-09-25T124442)

Destroyer 0.3.0 (engine-v2) as White, 42 moves, checkmate. Reviewed from the depth-18 full-strength eval pass (`.evals.json`); one 2s `bin/stockfish` probe (1 thread available on this box) used to sanity-check the one critical alternative line (move 16) — see note under moment B.

## 1. Verdict

This wasn't a positional squeeze — it was a mutual near-blunder in the middlegame that happened to break our way, followed by a clean if unspectacular technical conversion. White's 16.axb5 (recapturing the wrong way) let Black's queen leap to b4 with real threats and briefly gave Black a near-winning position (+113cp for Black). Black then returned the favor immediately with 16...Qb4??, walking into 17.Ra4! — a fork that only exists because the a-file happened to be open. From there Black's knight got stuck shuffling on b2/d3 for twenty moves while White's rooks ate the queenside alive and marched to mate. We won because our tactics-finding (quiescence + MVV-LVA) is solid at short range and Stockfish-2000 makes bigger unforced errors under a cramped, pieceless position than we do — not because our positional understanding is ahead of theirs.

## 2. Critical moments

### A. Move 13, 13.dxe5 (ply 25) — tactics covering for missing prophylaxis
**FEN before:** `r2qk2r/1p2bpp1/2pp1n1p/pP2p3/P1nPP3/B1N2B2/2P2PPP/R2Q1RK1 w kq - 1 13`
Black's 12...Nc4 attacks Ba3. We played **13.dxe5**, ignoring the hanging bishop entirely (drop 71cp vs. best). It only works because the engine's 2-ply search finds 14.Bxe7! as a zwischenzug — trading the attacked bishop off before Nxa3 is possible — so the piece never actually falls.
**What I'd play:** 13.Bc1, simply retreating. Keeps the same structural plan, doesn't burn a tempo on a "does the tactic work" gamble, and any strong opponent who finds 13...Nxa3!? intending 14.exf6 gxf6/Qxf6 with an extra exchange for a pawn puts us to a much harder test than we needed to take.

### B. Move 16, 16.axb5 vs. 16.Nxb5 (ply 31) — the real turning point
**FEN before:** `r3k2r/1p2qpp1/5n1p/pp2p3/P1n1P3/2NQ1B2/2P2PPP/R4RK1 w kq - 0 16`
Black just played 15...cxb5, doubling on the queenside. We recaptured **16.axb5** (drop 93cp — the single biggest error we made all game). Best was **16.Nxb5**.
**What I'd play:** 16.Nxb5. It keeps the a-pawn as a shield/lever, keeps the knight off the back rank where it can be harassed, and critically it does **not** hand Black's queen a free tempo-gaining jump to b4 hitting an undefended Nc3. After the game move, Black's position briefly reached +113 — essentially winning — for the cost of one square of recapture choice. This is the moment that would decide a rematch against a 2200 that doesn't immediately give the advantage back.
*Live check: a 2s/1-thread `bin/stockfish` probe from this exact FEN (depth ~6, so read directionally, not for the exact numbers) scored 16.Nxb5 at −13 and 16.axb5 at −38, confirming Nxb5 is the sounder recapture; it also flagged 16.Be2 (declining to recapture immediately) as better still, at +11 — worth a look if this pattern recurs.*

### C. Move 16...Qb4?? / 17.Ra4! (plies 32–33) — both sides err, fortune flips
**FEN before Qb4:** `r3k2r/1p2qpp1/5n1p/pP2p3/2n1P3/2NQ1B2/2P2PPP/R4RK1 b kq - 0 16`
Black had Rc8 available, keeping a near-decisive edge (+113). Instead 16...Qb4?? overextends the queen. We found **17.Ra4!** immediately (matches best move, essentially perfect) — a rook lift down the file that axb5 had (accidentally) opened, hitting the queen. After 17...Nb2 18.Rxb4 Nxd3, queens come off and Black's knight is stranded on the rim. This is the one moment our calculation is genuinely praiseworthy: Ra4 is not an obvious human move at first glance (rook swings behind its own pawns), and the engine found it at full value.

### D. Move 19...Nb2?? / 20.Rc7! (plies 38–39) — Black's piece dies of loneliness
**FEN before Nb2:** `r3k2r/1p3pp1/5n1p/pP2p3/2R1P3/2Nn1B2/2P2PPP/5RK1 b kq - 1 19`
Black's knight, having just captured our queen on d3, retreats to b2 again (repeating a square it already visited on move 17) instead of the far more active 19...Nf4 (drop 132cp — Black's worst move of the game). We answered with **20.Rc7!**, the correct and decisive choice (matches best): the rook goes to the 7th rank while Black's own piece is offside and can't contest it. From here it's a one-way street — the knight is captured on move 30 (Rxb2) having made no further contribution, and White's rooks strip the queenside bare.
**What I'd play:** exactly Rc7 — no improvement available. Flagging this moment because it's the point the game becomes technically won, not because of anything we could do better.

### E. Endgame technique, moves 29–37 (plies 57, 63, 73) — small conversion leaks
Representative FEN (move 29, ply 57): `1r1r2k1/1PR2ppn/7p/1B2p3/p3P3/2N5/1nP2PPP/R5K1 w - - 3 29` — we played 29.Ra2 (drop 71cp; best was Nxa4, grabbing a free pawn and centralizing). Similar small drops recur at move 32 (32.Rxf7 over Bc4, drop 38cp) and move 21 (21.b6 over Nd5, drop 66cp). None of these mattered — we were already +400 to +700cp — but they're the same pattern each time: **greedy/obvious rook moves over the more forcing centralizing piece move.** This is exactly what the queued mop-up/endgame term is meant to fix, and this game is a clean data point for it.

## 3. Recurring weaknesses (even in a win)

1. **No hanging-piece/SEE awareness outside quiescence.** Move 13 (dxe5) walked past an attacked bishop and was saved only because the save was inside the search's horizon. Against a deeper-thinking opponent this is a coin flip, not a plan.
2. **Recapture-choice blindness.** 16.axb5 vs. Nxb5 is a textbook "which piece recaptures" decision — the two options are materially identical but positionally very different (tempo, king safety, file control). This cost 93cp and nearly handed Black a winning game. This is a search-depth problem (needs to look past the immediate recapture to the resulting piece activity), not an eval-term problem.
3. **No plan against an uncastled king.** Black didn't castle until move 20. Our engine had no way to value that (no king-safety/file-openness term), so instead of a direct central plan it spent moves 6–15 on a slow, only loosely justified queenside pawn storm (b4/a4, 84cp of cumulative drop between the two) that worked here mostly because Stockfish-2000 didn't punish it.
4. **Opening-phase bleed dwarfs mid/endgame bleed.** The first 10 White moves alone cost **213cp of cumulative eval loss** — more than the entire rest of the 42-move game combined — against a 9cp/move game average. We have no book, so every opening decision (Bd3, Nc3 blocking c3, b4, a4) is a PST-guided guess instead of known theory.
5. **No trapped-piece exploitation.** Once Black's knight was stuck on b2/d3 for ~20 plies (moves 19–30), our engine never specifically hunted it — it kept making ordinary, only-slightly-suboptimal rook moves elsewhere (see 2E) instead of moves that immediately restrict or win the trapped piece.

## 4. Concrete suggestions, ranked by expected impact

1. **[NEW] Add a static hanging-piece/SEE safety check to move selection**, independent of quiescence depth: for every one of our own pieces on a square where opponent-attackers ≥ defenders, subtract the piece's value (or run a proper SEE) before finalizing the root move. Move 13 (13.dxe5) only survived because the saving tactic happened to be inside the search horizon; this term makes that no longer a gamble. Cheapest fix here, highest immediate payoff against an opponent that doesn't recapture the way we expect.
2. **[ALREADY QUEUED] Transposition table + PVS/LMR.** The 16.axb5-vs-Nxb5 decision (93cp, the single worst error of the game) is a "which recapture leaves better piece activity" question that needs real extra plies to resolve, not a hand-written eval term — it's a search-depth problem. This game is direct evidence for prioritizing it: our worst blunder was a positional recapture choice, not a tactical miss.
3. **[NEW] Piece-mobility / trapped-piece term in eval.** A simple legal-move count (or "attacked and has ≤1 safe square") bonus would have let the engine specifically go after Black's stranded knight on b2/d3 instead of making generic rook moves elsewhere (2E). This generalizes: exploiting a trapped piece converts advantages faster and more forcingly than shuffling toward a slow pawn endgame.
4. **[ALREADY QUEUED] Opening book (~10 ply, White lines).** 213cp of this game's total eval loss — more than half the entire game's bleed — came from the first 10 White moves. A book removes this whole category outright and gets us into known, sound structures before the search has to improvise.
5. **[ALREADY QUEUED] Mop-up endgame term / passed pawns / king safety (pawn shield).** Covers both the small conversion leaks in 2E and the "no plan against an uncastled king" weakness (3.3) — didn't cost us this particular game since the material edge was overwhelming by move 25, but it's the difference between a 20-move technical grind and a 10-move forced collapse against tougher opposition.
