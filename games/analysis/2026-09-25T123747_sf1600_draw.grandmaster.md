# Grandmaster Review — Destroyer 0.2.0 vs Stockfish 1600 (Draw, 203 plies)

Engine: Destroyer 0.2.0 (engine-v1: alpha-beta + quiescence + MVV-LVA, material-only eval, no repetition detection). We were White.

## 1. Verdict

We were strategically dead by move 24 and should have lost this game outright — three separate rook/piece-trade blunders (moves 24, 35, then the 59/60 combo) handed Black a winning material edge, and at move 65 we walked the king into a position with a *forced mate* that only survived because the Elo-1600 opponent didn't find the cleanest continuation. The draw was earned in the last ten moves, not the first hundred: a genuine fortress — a knight permanently guarding a pawn one square from queening, with our king shuffling between two squares that dodge every check — held a position material-only eval still calls a loss. This was a save, not a good game.

## 2. Critical moments

### Move 24. Rxd8 (best: Rd6)
`3r1rk1/pb3ppp/4pn2/P1P1n3/5qP1/1PP2P1P/2BRQK2/1N4NR w - - 5 24`
Eval: -86 → -391

Our rook on h1 hasn't moved once in 24 moves. Black's queen, both knights, and bishop are all active; ours are cramped and undeveloped. In that situation you do not simplify — trading the only pair of rooks on the board hands Black a clean, technical, low-risk conversion instead of a messy position they'd have to work for. Rd6 keeps tension, eyes e6/Nf6, and refuses to let Black cash in their activity edge for a risk-free ending. This is the single move that turned "roughly equal, unclear" into "objectively lost."

### Move 35. Bxc4 (best: c7)
`3r2k1/p4pp1/P1P1n3/3bp1qp/1Pn5/1BP2P1P/4Q3/1N3KNR w - - 0 35`
Eval: -485 → -626

Our c-pawn has been sitting on c6 — one push from c7, two from queening — since move 32, completely unmolested. A knight lands on c4 attacking our bishop, and the engine reflexively recaptures material instead of pushing c7, which forces an immediate Rc8 and starts consuming Black's clock and attention on the one thing that actually decides these positions: a pawn about to become a queen. Trading a bishop for a knight is a footnote next to that. This is "resolve the local threat" thinking beating "play your trump" thinking.

### Move 59. Na3 (best: Nc3)
`6k1/p7/Pq6/1P5p/2P2p2/5P2/3R1NK1/1N2r3 w - - 0 59`
Eval: -666 → -993

Already lost on the scoreboard (Q+R vs R+2N), but not yet hopeless — the a6/b5 pawns are real currency and the position calls for coordinating pieces around the king. Nc3 centralizes, covers e4/d5, and denies the coming Qg6+ ideas. Na3 puts the same knight on another dead rim square, attacking nothing, defending nothing relevant to the king. One tempo later Black's Rc1 hits the other knight and the Qg6+ fork idea is live. A knight shuffled from one corner to another while your king is about to come under fire is a wasted move you cannot afford here.

### Move 60. Rd5 (best: Nd1)
`6k1/p7/Pq6/1P5p/2P2p2/N4P2/3R1NK1/2r5 w - - 2 60`
Eval: -516 → -847

Black's Rc1 is attacking the loose Nb1; the position has a live check-and-fork threat (Qg6+) about to land. Nd1 retreats the attacked piece to safety and covers key squares. Rd5 does neither — it walks the rook to a square that does nothing concrete while ignoring both the attacked knight and the coming fork. Two moves later, 60...Qg6+ forks king and rook and we're down a full rook on top of everything else. Solve the concrete threat on the board before making a "nice" positional move — the engine's un-extended search simply didn't see the fork two plies out.

### Move 65. Kxf4 (best: Ke4)
`8/p4k2/P7/1P5q/2PR1p2/N4K2/8/2r5 w - - 1 65`
Eval: -904 → -9989 (forced mate)

Already down a rook and objectively finished, but *not yet mated*. Grabbing the free pawn on f4 looks natural, but it puts the king on the same file as Black's rook and the same diagonal as Black's queen simultaneously — a battery the king can't escape. Ke4 steps toward the center and off both lines, staying a tempo ahead of the mating net. When you're already lost, the rule is king safety over any free pawn — never walk into a file/diagonal shared by two enemy heavy pieces. We only survived because the 1600-strength opponent didn't find the cleanest mating line (it played Rf1+ then drifted); a stronger opponent ends the game right here.

### Moves 95–102 — the fortress that actually drew the game
`8/pP6/P1kN4/2P5/K2q4/8/8/8` (final repeating position, White to move)

By this point material is Q+P vs N+3P — crushing for Black on paper, and full-strength Stockfish's own static eval still says -591. It's a dead draw anyway: the b7-pawn is one square from queening and is permanently guarded by Nd6 (Kxb7 would move the king into a square the knight attacks, which is illegal), so Black's king is glued to the queenside forever babysitting a pawn it can't touch. White's king has exactly two squares (a4/b3) that dodge every check the queen can give from d4/e3, and neither side can make progress — a textbook two-square perpetual-check shuffle. This is a real, sound fortress that our own earlier choices (pushing the connected a/b pawns, routing the knight to d6) built, even though v1 had zero repetition awareness and didn't "know" it was heading anywhere — it held simply because the individual defensive moves were correct.

## 3. Recurring weaknesses

- **Reflex captures over plans.** Twice (moves 24, 35) the engine resolved a small local issue (a rook offered for trade, a piece under attack) by capturing/trading instead of recognizing a much bigger asset on the board — an actively favorable pawn or a lead in activity that a trade would throw away.
- **Knight parked on the rim under pressure.** Na3 (59), Nc1 (84), Nb1 (91, again 93) — the same defensive knight repeatedly retreats to a corner square instead of a central one, exactly when the king needs it for cover. (Root cause was material-only eval; already fixed by the PST added in v2 — flagging it here because it's the dominant visible pattern in this specific game.)
- **King walks toward danger when already lost.** Kxf4 at move 65 grabbed material while opening a file/diagonal battery. There's no general "don't increase your own king's check exposure" instinct once the position is already bad — only material counting.
- **Passed pawns treated as static, not as a plan.** The c-pawn sat on c6, one push from c7, for roughly 15 moves while pieces traded around it; "best" moves flagged b5/a6 pushes repeatedly that weren't played. The pawns that eventually saved the game were advanced too slowly to matter for the middlegame.
- **No damage-limitation mode.** Between move 59 and move 95 the engine racked up six more tagged blunders (Na3, Rd5, Rd3, Kc2, Nc1, Kd3, Nb1, c5) in a position that was already gone. It kept playing "best local move" rather than shifting to minimizing further losses once the outcome was effectively decided.

## 4. Concrete suggestions (ranked by expected impact)

1. **Add an opponent-model complexity bias when the engine is significantly behind.** When post-search eval is worse than roughly -300cp, break near-ties in root move selection in favor of the candidate that keeps more material/tension on the board and maximizes the opponent's branching factor, instead of the "cleanest" simplifying line. Our opponent is a fixed, Elo-limited Stockfish that reliably misplays complicated positions — in this single game Black gave back 100–9000+ cp on roughly ten of its own moves (moves 59, 71, 75, 89, and others). A shallow material-only or PST eval can't tell "clean lost" from "messy lost," but messy is what wins points against this specific opponent. This is the highest-impact, most mission-specific change and isn't covered by anything currently queued.

2. **Scale the v3 draw contempt to how bad our own position is, instead of a flat 30cp.** Once our eval drops below roughly -400/-500cp, sharply increase the value the search assigns to repetition-reachable or fortress-like continuations so it actively steers toward them, rather than only detecting a repetition once the position has already drifted there. This game shows the engine bled six further tagged blunders across ~35 plies (moves 59–95) in a position that was already lost before it happened to stumble into the drawing fortress at the end. A loss-scaled contempt would make it seek that outcome on purpose and stop shedding material once further loss can no longer change the result.

3. **Add a cheap king-danger term for queen-vs-no-queen endgames: count squares/lines from which the enemy queen currently checks or could check next move, and penalize king moves that increase that count.** This is a static eval signal, not a search-depth fix — it would have flagged Kxf4 (move 65) as worse than Ke4 immediately, without needing the search to find the forced mate four plies deep. Distinct from the already-queued "king safety (pawn shield)" item, which targets castled-king pawn structure in the middlegame, not a bare king dodging queen checks in an ending.

4. **Add a "passer escort" bonus: reward king/knight proximity to our own advanced passed pawns.** Several late blunders (Nb1 at moves 91 and 93, the king detour at move 86) walked the defending pieces away from exactly the pawns (a6/b5/c-pawn→b7) that ended up saving the game. This builds on top of the already-queued generic "passed pawns" eval term rather than duplicating it — it specifically rewards the escort/blockade relationship, which is what actually built the fortress at the end of this game.
