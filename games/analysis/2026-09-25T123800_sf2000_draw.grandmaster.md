# Grandmaster review — 2026-09-25T123800_sf2000_draw (Destroyer 0.2.0 / engine-v1, White, vs SF 2000)

## Verdict

This was not a well-defended draw — it was a gift. In the final third of the game White (us) was down a queen for a rook and knight in a completely lost endgame (final eval −737, having touched mate scores twice), and only survived because SF-2000 repeatedly failed to convert and eventually let its king get shuffled into a threefold repetition. Our own play contributed a steady stream of small-to-medium errors from the opening (weakening pawn pushes instead of developing pieces — material-only eval had no way to know better), but the story that's *new* here is four separate moments where our king walked forward on its own into danger: twice it walked into a mating attack that the weakened opponent then failed to execute, and once it walked away from a piece it was defending and simply dropped a rook for free.

## Critical moments

### 1. Ply 13, 1...Qe7 2.f3?! (best: Nd2)
FEN before: `rnb1k1nr/ppp1qppp/3b4/8/4p3/PPP1P3/5PPP/RNBQKBNR w KQkq - 1 7`

We played **f3**, engine's evaluation swings from −95 to −237. This is the moment the game's whole character is set. White had already burned three tempi on a3/b3/c3 and was behind in development; **Nd2** develops a piece, prepares Ngf3 or Nxe4, and doesn't touch the kingside pawn structure that the king will eventually need. f3 instead commits to a second consecutive pawn move, permanently weakens e3/g3, and buys nothing — it doesn't even win back the e4 pawn cleanly. This single habit (push a pawn instead of develop a piece) repeats at 17.g3 (best Nf3, −148) and 19.a4 (best Bg2, −212), and by move 20 White is already down over five pawns of engine-eval while still not castled. All three moves are symptoms of material-only evaluation: there's no bonus for king safety or piece activity to warn the search off them. That specific defect is already fixed by engine-v2's PST — worth spot-checking these exact three FENs against the current build to confirm.

### 2. Ply 47, 24.Ke2?? (best: Kd2) — walked into a mate
FEN before: `1b1r2k1/pp2rpp1/5q2/5b1p/P7/B1PnP1P1/6QP/RN2K1NR w KQ - 0 24`

White is in check from ...Nd3+ and the position is already lost (−1021), but not every king move is equally lost. **Kd2** tucks the king one file away from the center of the action; **Ke2**, what we played, keeps the king in the direct line of fire of Black's queen (f6), dark bishop (f5, eyeing the long diagonal into the king's shelter), and the rook already on e7. The engine's own analysis confirms it: after Ke2, Black has a forced mate (eval crashes to a mate score) starting with **...Rxe3+!**, a rook sacrifice that tears open the position around our king with queen, bishop and knight all already in position. SF-2000 didn't find it — it played the far weaker ...Bd6 and let the evaluation fall back to a merely-lost −831 — but the point stands: our king, told to pick among legal replies to a check, chose the one that loses by force. This is a *new* failure mode this game, distinct from the material-only eval problem above: it's not "doesn't value king safety in general," it's "when forced to respond to a check, doesn't see far enough ahead to know which reply is fatal."

### 3. Ply 83–88: 42.Kf1–43.Kg1–44.Kf1?? Bxh1 — dropped a whole rook by walking away from it
FEN before the losing move (44.Kf1): `4R3/pp3ppk/8/3b4/P5Np/2P3P1/3q3P/6KR w - - 11 44`

This is the cleanest, most human-explainable blunder in the game. On move 43, King g1 sits adjacent to, and therefore defends, our own rook on h1. Black retreats the bishop to d5 (still eyeing the long a8–h1 diagonal, which is completely empty: e4, f3, g2 are all clear). Instead of leaving the king in place or playing **Nf2** (the engine's own suggested best move, which keeps something covering the back rank), we play **Kf1** — moving the *only* defender of h1 off the square that defends it. Black replies **Bxh1** and simply wins the rook for nothing; the eval crater (1097 → 724 cp swing for Black, i.e. a full rook) is instant, one-ply, no combination required. This isn't a deep tactic we're too shallow to see — the position eval already registered Kf1 as bad the moment it was played (−1097), which means the search *did* compute the cost correctly; it just failed to search the alternative (Nf2) deeply/consistently enough to prefer it. That points at move-ordering/search-efficiency (no transposition table, no killer/history heuristics, no PVS/LMR — all already queued) rather than at evaluation.

### 4. Ply 113, 57.Kf3?? (best: Rd3) — the same pattern again
FEN before: `8/p4p1k/P6P/1p4p1/2b5/6R1/4KN1P/2q5 w - - 0 57`

White is in check from ...Bc4+. **Rd3** blocks the check cleanly and keeps the king out of the firing line. We instead walk the king to **Kf3** — again choosing a king move over an interposition when the position is already materially lost (rook + knight vs. queen + pawns) — and again the engine's own analysis says this loses by force (mate score after Kf3). SF-2000 again failed to find the cleanest continuation, playing ...Qf4+ instead of the machine-best follow-up, and the position "only" settled at a crushing but non-mating −1213. Two occurrences of the identical pattern (walk the king into a losing attack instead of taking the safe interposition/retreat) in one game is a signal, not a coincidence.

### 5. Ply 189–207: R+N vs Q+2 connected passed pawns — held only because Black couldn't finish
FEN at the start of this stretch: `8/8/5k2/5p2/8/p3N3/6R1/2q1K3 w - - 4 95`

By this point material is King+Rook+Knight vs King+Queen+two connected passed pawns (a- and f-pawns) — a textbook, comfortably winning endgame for Black by any human or engine standard. Destroyer had no way to actively seek a draw here: engine-v1 has no repetition detection, so any drawing outcome from this position was accidental, not a defensive plan. What actually happened is instructive: our rook kept giving checks/hitting the queen (Ra2, Rg2+, Re2+) while the black king shuffled among a small handful of squares (f6, e6/e7/d6, g5, f5, h6) without a concrete winning plan, and the position eventually recurred three times. That's a full-point escape manufactured entirely by the opponent's own inaccuracy at Elo 2000, not by any resourcefulness on our side.

## Recurring weaknesses in our engine's play

- **No king-safety awareness when choosing among check-responses.** Three of the four king moves examined above (Ke2, Kf1, Kf3) picked the losing or material-dropping option among legal replies to a check. This is new and specific — narrower than "the king should generally be safe," it's "which of these 2–3 legal moves is the one that doesn't lose by force," and it happened repeatedly.
- **Blind to a piece becoming undefended by its own king's move.** The Bxh1 loss is a one-ply tactic our own search's evaluation clearly flagged after the fact but still played into — a search-consistency problem, not an evaluation problem.
- **No plan in the middlegame beyond local material/tactics.** Qe2→Qa2→Qg2, Bf1 (retreating a developed bishop) — the queen and bishop get shuffled reactively rather than pursuing development or an idea. Largely a material-only-eval symptom (queued/fixed by PST going forward, worth re-verifying).
- **No instinct to seek a draw when clearly lost.** Until v3, the engine had no concept that a repetition might be desirable; the escape in this game was luck, not technique.
- **Can't punish the opponent's own big mistakes.** SF-2000 handed away a forced mate twice (drop_cp 8967 and 8769 on its next moves) and our engine, already deep in a lost position of its own making, had nothing prepared to exploit either window.

## Concrete suggestions (ranked by expected impact)

1. **Prioritize check extensions now (already queued) — this game is direct evidence.** Both mate-score blunders (Ke2 at ply 47, Kf3 at ply 113) happened exactly when the side to move was in check and had to pick among a small set of legal replies; a check extension gives the search one more ply of look-ahead right where it matters most, which is precisely what would let it distinguish "Kd2 is fine" from "Ke2 loses by force." Move it to the top of the queue.
2. **Add an explicit king-exposure term for evaluating check-responses, distinct from the queued pawn-shield king-safety term.** The pawn-shield formulation is aimed at castled/endgame structures and won't fire in these FENs — there's no pawn shield to speak of, the issue is a bare king in the center of an open board with enemy queen+minor pieces already in range. Score legal king moves (and interpositions) by counting enemy attackers/open lines to the resulting king square, and regression-test against the two FENs above (ply 47 and ply 113) — the fix should make Kd2 and Rd3 clearly preferred.
3. **Add a cheap "does this move undefend one of my own pieces" check (SEE-based) to move selection.** This is what actually cost us the rook on h1: Kf1 abandoned the only defender of h1, and the resulting Bxh1 was a one-ply, non-tactical loss that better move-ordering/search consistency (also served by the already-queued TT+hash-move and PVS/LMR work) would very likely have caught by searching Nf2 to comparable depth. Worth treating as its own regression test in addition to relying on the general search-quality improvements.
4. **Make the repetition/contempt handling loss-aware.** Engine-v3's 30cp contempt (already fixed) presumably discourages repetition uniformly; but this exact game shows a lost position (R+N vs Q+2P) where actively steering toward a repetition is the *correct* plan. Flip the contempt sign (or scale it toward zero/negative) once static eval drops below some large loss threshold, so the engine starts to *seek* draws instead of merely tolerating them when it's clearly losing — and confirm it wouldn't now avoid the very repetition that saved this game.
